4 Human and Organizational Readiness Framework
===============================================

4.1 Overview
------------

This chapter defines the proposed Human and Organizational Readiness Framework for Trusted Agentic AI (HORA-TAI).

The framework provides a structured approach for assessing whether the human and organizational conditions surrounding a defined delegation of authority are sufficient to support trustworthy operation of an agentic AI system.

The framework is based on the principle that the appropriate level of delegated authority depends not only on agent capability, but also on the ability of humans and organizations to understand, supervise, govern, intervene in, and remain accountable for agent activity.

The framework therefore evaluates readiness across multiple dimensions.

Readiness is not treated as a permanent characteristic of an individual or organization. It is assessed in relation to a specific delegation context.

4.2 Framework Model
-------------------

The HORA-TAI framework consists of the following core components:

* delegation context;
* delegated authority;
* readiness dimensions;
* delegation level;
* human oversight;
* organizational governance;
* accountability and traceability;
* assurance evidence; and
* continuous reassessment.

These components are interdependent.

A delegation decision should consider the relationship between:

**Agent capability + Delegated authority + Human readiness + Organizational readiness + Oversight capability + Assurance**

A high-capability agent does not by itself establish that a delegation is appropriate.

Similarly, a highly capable organization may not be ready to delegate a particular activity if the agent lacks sufficient capability, safeguards, or controllability.


The framework distinguishes three assessment domains:

* **Agent capability:** whether the agent can perform the assigned activity within the required technical and operational constraints.
* **Human readiness:** whether the responsible humans can understand, supervise, challenge, and intervene in the agent's activity.
* **Organizational readiness:** whether the organization can authorize, govern, monitor, support, and remain accountable for the delegation.

These domains should be examined separately before they are considered together in a delegation decision. Strength in one domain does not compensate automatically for a material weakness in another.

4.3 Readiness Dimensions
------------------------

The framework proposes eight primary readiness dimensions:

1. Human Capability and Understanding
2. Meaningful Human Oversight
3. Delegation Governance
4. Accountability and Responsibility
5. Authorization and Boundary Management
6. Workforce and Organizational Capability
7. Traceability and Assurance
8. Continuous Monitoring and Adaptation

Each dimension should be assessed in relation to the specific delegation context.

4.3.1 Human Capability and Understanding
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This dimension addresses whether the relevant human actors possess sufficient knowledge and capability to understand and appropriately interact with the agentic AI system.

Assessment may consider:

* understanding of the agent's purpose and capabilities;
* understanding of relevant limitations and failure modes;
* ability to interpret agent outputs and actions;
* awareness of material risks;
* ability to recognize unexpected or anomalous behavior;
* knowledge of intervention procedures;
* ability to exercise appropriate judgment; and
* familiarity with applicable policies and responsibilities.

Human capability should be proportionate to the authority delegated and the consequences of agent activity.

4.3.2 Meaningful Human Oversight
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This dimension addresses whether human oversight is substantive and effective rather than merely procedural.

Assessment may consider:

* whether relevant agent activity is visible to the responsible human;
* whether the human receives sufficient information to exercise judgment;
* whether intervention is technically possible;
* whether intervention can occur within an appropriate timeframe;
* whether the responsible human has authority to intervene;
* whether workload permits effective supervision;
* whether escalation mechanisms are available; and
* whether oversight remains effective as agent autonomy increases.

The presence of a human in a workflow should not, by itself, be considered evidence of meaningful human oversight.

4.3.3 Delegation Governance
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This dimension addresses whether the organization has appropriate processes for deciding when, why, and under what conditions authority may be delegated to an agentic AI system.

Assessment may consider:

* defined delegation policies;
* approval processes;
* risk assessment procedures;
* role and responsibility definitions;
* delegation criteria;
* escalation requirements;
* suspension and termination procedures;
* change-management processes; and
* governance oversight.

Delegation governance should establish who is authorized to grant, modify, review, and terminate delegated authority.

4.3.4 Accountability and Responsibility
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This dimension addresses whether responsibility for delegated activities and outcomes remains identifiable.

Assessment may consider:

* identification of the delegating party;
* identification of the supervising human or function;
* identification of operational responsibilities;
* assignment of decision rights;
* incident ownership;
* escalation responsibility;
* review responsibility; and
* applicable legal, regulatory, and organizational obligations.

The use of an AI agent should not create an accountability gap.

4.3.5 Authorization and Boundary Management
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This dimension addresses whether the authority granted to an agent is explicit, appropriately limited, and technically and organizationally controllable.

Assessment may consider:

* defined permissions;
* permitted and prohibited actions;
* transaction or operational limits;
* access boundaries;
* temporal limitations;
* geographic or organizational boundaries where applicable;
* approval requirements;
* escalation thresholds;
* emergency controls; and
* authority revocation mechanisms.

Delegated authority should be no broader than necessary to achieve the defined objective.

4.3.6 Workforce and Organizational Capability
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This dimension addresses whether the organization has the workforce capabilities and organizational resources required to operate and supervise agentic AI.

Assessment may consider:

* AI literacy;
* role-specific training;
* technical expertise;
* governance expertise;
* risk-management capabilities;
* operational support;
* incident-response capabilities;
* staffing levels;
* workload allocation; and
* organizational change-management capability.

Workforce readiness should be considered across the full lifecycle of agentic AI deployment rather than only during initial implementation.

4.3.7 Traceability and Assurance Evidence
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This dimension addresses whether relevant agent activity can be sufficiently reconstructed and whether the resulting records and other evidence can support evaluation of the delegation.

Traceability concerns the ability to reconstruct relevant events and relationships. Assurance concerns the evaluation of evidence to establish confidence that specified controls, capabilities, and governance arrangements are present and operating as intended. The two concepts are related but should not be treated as interchangeable.

Assessment may consider:

* records of delegated authority;
* agent instructions and objectives;
* authorization information;
* significant agent decisions;
* material actions;
* external interactions;
* human interventions;
* exceptions and escalations;
* outcomes; and
* assurance evidence.

The level of traceability should be proportionate to the nature and consequences of the delegated activity.

4.3.8 Continuous Monitoring and Adaptation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

This dimension addresses whether readiness and delegation remain appropriate as conditions change.

Assessment may consider:

* changes in agent capabilities;
* changes in operating environments;
* changes in organizational structures;
* changes in workforce capabilities;
* changes in applicable requirements;
* changes in risk;
* performance trends;
* incidents and near misses;
* emerging failure modes; and
* changes in delegated authority.

Continuous assurance should provide mechanisms for reducing, modifying, suspending, or terminating delegation when readiness conditions are no longer satisfied.

Delegation readiness is not determined by agent capability alone.
A technically capable or trustworthy agent does not, by itself,
establish that a human or organization is ready to accept the
authority associated with a particular delegation.

Delegation readiness is a socio-technical property of the
relationship between agent capability, delegated authority, human
capability, organizational capability, meaningful human oversight,
accountability, controls, consequences, reversibility, and assurance
evidence.

Accordingly, the determination of whether a delegation should proceed
should consider both the characteristics of the agentic AI system and
the readiness of the humans and organization responsible for the
delegation.

4.4 Delegation and Readiness Relationship
-----------------------------------------

The relationship between delegated authority and readiness is central to the framework.

As the scope, autonomy, complexity, or consequence of delegated authority increases, the required level of human and organizational readiness may also increase.

The framework therefore does not define readiness independently of delegation.

Instead:

**Required readiness = f(delegated authority, autonomy, consequence, reversibility, operating context, and available controls)**

This relationship should be interpreted qualitatively during the initial development of the framework and may subsequently be supported by empirical assessment methods.

4.5 Proposed Delegation Levels
------------------------------

For the purposes of this specification, five descriptive delegation levels are proposed.

These levels are intended to provide a common vocabulary for discussing the relative degree of authority delegated to an agentic AI system.

They are not intended to constitute a universal risk classification.

4.5.1 Level 0 - Advisory
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

At Level 0, the agent provides information, analysis, recommendations, or proposed actions.

The human remains responsible for deciding whether and how to act.

The agent does not independently execute consequential actions.

Examples may include:

* generating recommendations;
* summarizing information;
* identifying potential options;
* drafting content; and
* proposing actions for human review.

4.5.2 Level 1 - Constrained Execution
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

At Level 1, the agent may execute narrowly defined actions within explicit constraints.

Human authorization establishes the permitted activity, and the agent operates within predetermined boundaries.

Examples may include:

* submitting routine administrative requests;
* executing predefined software or workflow tasks;
* updating approved records; and
* initiating low-consequence operational activities.

4.5.3 Level 2 - Conditional Autonomy
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

At Level 2, the agent may make intermediate decisions and execute multiple actions without obtaining human approval for every individual step, provided that predefined conditions are satisfied.

Human oversight remains available and defined escalation conditions apply.

Examples may include:

* resolving routine customer-service cases;
* coordinating predefined business workflows;
* executing multi-step administrative processes; and
* selecting among predefined operational options.

4.5.4 Level 3 - Extended Autonomy
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

At Level 3, the agent may pursue a defined objective through multiple steps and make broader intermediate decisions with limited direct human intervention.

The organization should establish stronger governance, monitoring, intervention, and assurance mechanisms.

Examples may include:

* managing complex operational workflows;
* coordinating multiple software tools or services;
* optimizing defined business processes; and
* executing extended sequences of actions within a defined operational domain.

4.5.5 Level 4 - Advanced Delegated Authority
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

At Level 4, the agent may exercise substantial delegated authority and broad autonomy within a defined operational context. The level describes the scope and degree of delegated authority; it does not classify the potential consequence of the activity.

Level 4 requires particularly strong consideration of:

* human oversight;
* intervention capability;
* authorization;
* accountability;
* traceability;
* assurance;
* reversibility;
* escalation; and
* organizational readiness.

A Level 4 delegation is not automatically appropriate. Its suitability depends on the assessed consequence, reversibility, intervention conditions, readiness, and assurance evidence.

Delegation Decision Factors
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

A delegation level should not be interpreted as a standalone
determination of whether a delegation is appropriate. The delegation
decision should consider the relationship among delegation level,
consequence, reversibility, time available for human intervention,
human readiness, organizational readiness, and available assurance
evidence.

The relationship may be represented conceptually as:

::

   Delegation Level
          +
      Consequence
          +
      Reversibility
          +
   Intervention Conditions
          +
    Human Readiness
          +
  Organizational Readiness
          +
    Assurance Evidence
          =
    Delegation Decision

The factors should be assessed in the context of the intended use and
should be reassessed when the delegation context, agent capability,
organizational conditions, or consequences materially change.

For discussion, potential consequence may be described qualitatively as
limited, moderate, significant, or critical. These descriptors are
illustrative prompts rather than universal thresholds. Their
interpretation should reflect the affected parties, scale and duration
of potential effects, reversibility, and the operating context. They
should not be combined with the delegation level to produce an
unvalidated numerical score.

Delegation level describes the relative scope of authority and autonomy. Consequence describes the potential magnitude and significance of effects. A higher delegation level does not necessarily indicate a higher consequence, and a lower delegation level does not necessarily indicate a lower consequence. They should therefore be assessed as separate dimensions.

4.6 Readiness Assessment
------------------------

A readiness assessment should evaluate the relationship between the proposed delegation and the readiness dimensions defined in this chapter.

The assessment should consider at least:

* the objective of the delegation;
* the authority granted;
* the proposed delegation level;
* the agent's relevant capabilities and limitations;
* the consequences of incorrect or unintended actions;
* the reversibility of actions;
* the information and systems accessible to the agent;
* the available human oversight;
* intervention and escalation mechanisms;
* organizational governance;
* workforce capabilities;
* accountability arrangements;
* traceability mechanisms; and
* continuous assurance capabilities.

The assessment should identify both capabilities that are present and readiness gaps that may require remediation.

4.7 Readiness Determination
---------------------------

A readiness determination should result in one of the following types of outcomes:

* delegation may proceed under the defined conditions;
* delegation may proceed subject to specified controls or remediation;
* delegation authority should be reduced;
* additional human oversight should be established;
* additional organizational capabilities should be developed;
* delegation should be deferred pending remediation; or
* delegation should not proceed under the assessed conditions.

These outcomes are intended to support operational decision-making and are not intended to establish a universal certification scheme.

4.8 Readiness Evidence
----------------------

Readiness determinations should be supported by evidence appropriate to the delegation context.

Evidence may include:

* training records;
* competency assessments;
* governance policies;
* authorization records;
* risk assessments;
* oversight procedures;
* intervention tests;
* audit records;
* incident records;
* monitoring data;
* system documentation;
* assurance reports; and
* records of previous delegation decisions.

Evidence should be sufficiently reliable and current to support the relevant readiness determination.

4.9 Readiness Gaps and Remediation
----------------------------------

Where a readiness assessment identifies a gap, the organization should determine whether the gap can be addressed before delegation proceeds or whether delegated authority should be reduced.

Potential remediation measures include:

* additional human training;
* improved AI literacy;
* strengthened supervision;
* narrower delegation boundaries;
* additional approval requirements;
* stronger monitoring;
* improved traceability;
* enhanced incident response;
* additional technical safeguards;
* revised organizational responsibilities; and
* reassessment before increasing delegated authority.

4.10 Relationship Between Delegation and Oversight
---------------------------------------------------

Human oversight should be designed in relation to the actual authority and autonomy granted to the agent.

Oversight mechanisms may include:

* pre-action approval;
* approval of defined classes of actions;
* real-time monitoring;
* exception-based escalation;
* periodic review;
* post-action review;
* automated safeguards;
* authority suspension; and
* emergency intervention.

The appropriate combination depends on the delegation context.

Oversight should not be designed solely around the technical capabilities of the agent. It should also consider the ability of the responsible humans and organization to understand and exercise control over the agent.

4.11 Continuous Readiness
-------------------------

Readiness should not be treated as a one-time determination.

A previously acceptable delegation may become inappropriate when:

* agent capabilities change;
* the agent is connected to new tools or systems;
* the scope of authority expands;
* organizational responsibilities change;
* workforce capabilities change;
* operating conditions change;
* incidents reveal previously unidentified risks;
* applicable requirements change; or
* the consequences of agent actions change.

Organizations should therefore establish mechanisms for periodic and event-driven reassessment.

4.12 Framework Output
---------------------

The output of applying the HORA-TAI framework should provide a structured representation of:

* the delegation context;
* the delegated authority;
* the delegation level;
* the assessed readiness dimensions;
* identified readiness gaps;
* required controls;
* human oversight arrangements;
* accountability arrangements;
* assurance evidence;
* reassessment conditions; and
* the resulting delegation decision.

This output may subsequently be represented through a structured assurance record as described in the supporting annexes.

4.13 Future Development
-----------------------

The framework presented in this chapter is intended as an initial proposal for discussion.

Further development may include:

* empirical validation of readiness dimensions;
* development of quantitative or semi-quantitative assessment methods;
* testing across sectors and organizational contexts;
* development of maturity models;
* development of machine-readable assurance records;
* integration with identity and authorization infrastructures;
* development of interoperability mechanisms;
* analysis of workforce readiness indicators; and
* development of evidence-based thresholds for different delegation contexts.

Such development should be informed by research, practical deployments, stakeholder consultation, and experience from standards and assurance activities.

4.14 Relationship to Existing AI Governance Frameworks
------------------------------------------------------

HORA-TAI is intended to complement, rather than replace, existing AI
governance, risk-management, and assurance frameworks.

Existing frameworks may address organizational governance, AI risk,
system characteristics, accountability, transparency, safety, and
human oversight. HORA-TAI focuses specifically on the readiness of
humans and organizations to accept, supervise, govern, and remain
accountable for delegated authority to agentic AI systems.

Accordingly, HORA-TAI may be applied as a delegation-focused layer
within broader AI governance and risk-management processes.

The framework does not establish that a delegation is appropriate
solely because an AI system satisfies technical, governance, or
risk-management requirements. It considers whether the relevant human
and organizational conditions are sufficient for the authority being
delegated.

HORA-TAI therefore addresses a specific relationship between agentic
AI capability, delegated authority, human readiness, organizational
readiness, oversight, accountability, and assurance. The framework is
intended to support integration with existing governance and
assurance approaches while providing a distinct focus on the
conditions required for trustworthy delegation.


4.15 Assessment Criteria and Evidence
-------------------------------------

This section provides an initial assessment design for the eight readiness
dimensions. It is intended to make the dimensions more consistently
assessable without implying that the proposed criteria have been validated.

For each dimension, the assessment should identify the applicable criteria,
the evidence examined, any limitations in that evidence, the result, and any
required remediation. Criteria should be tailored to the delegation context;
not every item will apply to every use case.

The following result descriptors should be used for each applicable criterion:

* **Met:** sufficient, current, and relevant evidence supports the criterion.
* **Partially met:** some supporting arrangements or evidence exist, but a
  material limitation remains.
* **Not met:** the required capability, control, or arrangement is absent or
  demonstrably ineffective.
* **Not assessed:** the criterion has not been assessed or evidence is
  unavailable.

"Not assessed" should not be interpreted as "met". A criterion may be marked
not applicable only where the assessor records a reason linked to the
delegation context and the review authority accepts that rationale.

4.15.1 Human Capability and Understanding
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Assessment criteria should consider whether:

* each human role with oversight or intervention duties is identified;
* role holders can explain the agent's intended purpose and delegated
  authority;
* role holders understand material capabilities, limitations, and known
  failure modes relevant to their duties;
* role holders can interpret the information presented to them and recognize
  conditions requiring challenge or escalation;
* role holders have demonstrated the ability to use the applicable
  intervention and escalation procedures; and
* competency is reassessed when the agent, task, or delegation changes
  materially.

Potential evidence includes role-specific competency checks, scenario-based
exercises, training records, observed demonstrations, approved operating
procedures, and records of reassessment.

A material gap exists where a person expected to supervise or intervene
cannot understand the information needed for that role, cannot recognize
defined escalation conditions, or has not demonstrated the required
intervention capability. The delegation should not rely on untested
assumptions about human competence.

4.15.2 Meaningful Human Oversight
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Assessment criteria should consider whether:

* the responsible human or function is explicitly designated and available;
* the information needed to understand material agent activity is accessible
  in a usable form;
* the oversight role has sufficient authority, time, and resources;
* intervention can be initiated and completed within the time available
  before unacceptable effects occur;
* escalation routes reach a person or function with the authority and
  competence to act;
* monitoring and alerting arrangements have been tested under representative
  operating conditions; and
* oversight effectiveness is reviewed after material changes and incidents.

Potential evidence includes end-to-end intervention tests, alert and escalation
tests, workload analysis, monitoring records, operational simulations, and
documented oversight procedures.

A material gap exists where nominal human oversight cannot act in time, lacks
the authority to stop or modify activity, or is undermined by unavailable,
incomplete, or unintelligible information. A human approval step alone is not
evidence of meaningful oversight.

4.15.3 Delegation Governance
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Assessment criteria should consider whether:

* a documented delegation objective and scope have been approved;
* the approval authority is identified and authorized to grant the proposed
  delegation;
* the decision is informed by an assessment of agent capability, consequence,
  reversibility, and operating context;
* approval, review, change, suspension, and termination processes are
  documented;
* material changes to the agent, tools, data, environment, or authority trigger
  review before or when the change takes effect, as appropriate; and
* governance exceptions are recorded, reviewed, and resolved.

Potential evidence includes approved delegation policies, decision records,
risk assessments, change-control records, governance minutes, and suspension
or termination procedures.

A material gap exists where authority can be granted or expanded without an
identifiable approval process, where material changes bypass review, or where
the organization cannot suspend or terminate the delegation when required.

4.15.4 Accountability and Responsibility
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Assessment criteria should consider whether:

* a delegating party with authority to approve the delegation is identified;
* an operational owner is identified for the deployed agent and its operating
  environment;
* a supervising human or function is identified where oversight is required;
* an incident owner and escalation authority are identified;
* decision rights and hand-offs between roles are documented;
* role holders acknowledge and understand their assigned responsibilities;
  and
* records permit the organization to identify who authorized, operated,
  supervised, reviewed, and changed the delegation.

Potential evidence includes a responsibility assignment matrix, role
descriptions, approval records, operational procedures, incident plans, and
records of role acknowledgement.

A material gap exists where a critical decision, intervention, or incident
response has no assigned owner, where responsibilities conflict without a
resolution route, or where delegation obscures rather than clarifies
organizational accountability. This framework does not determine legal
liability or displace applicable law.

4.15.5 Authorization and Boundary Management
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Assessment criteria should consider whether:

* permitted and prohibited actions are explicitly defined;
* access is limited to the systems, data, tools, and operations necessary for
  the approved objective;
* transaction, volume, value, time, and other relevant limits are specified;
* controls prevent or detect actions outside the approved boundary;
* higher-impact or exceptional actions require the defined additional
  authorization;
* credentials, tokens, and delegated permissions can be revoked or
  constrained; and
* boundary controls are tested, monitored, and reviewed after material change.

Potential evidence includes access-control configurations, permission
inventories, policy tests, boundary-violation tests, approval logs, revocation
tests, and configuration records.

A material gap exists where the agent can exercise authority beyond the
approved scope, where restrictions are only advisory rather than enforced
where enforcement is required, or where authority cannot be promptly
constrained or revoked.

4.15.6 Workforce and Organizational Capability
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Assessment criteria should consider whether:

* the organization has identified the skills and functions needed across
  deployment, operation, oversight, security, risk, and incident response;
* staffing and on-call arrangements are sufficient for the expected operating
  pattern and intervention demands;
* personnel have role-appropriate training and access to specialist support;
* workload and competing duties do not make required oversight impracticable;
* operational support and incident response are available throughout the
  delegation period; and
* organizational changes that affect these capabilities trigger reassessment.

Potential evidence includes skills inventories, staffing and coverage plans,
training records, support agreements, workload assessments, incident exercises,
and organizational change records.

A material gap exists where the organization cannot provide the personnel,
skills, coverage, or operational support needed to supervise and respond to
the delegation. Planned but unfunded or unstaffed capabilities should not be
counted as established.

4.15.7 Traceability and Assurance Evidence
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Assessment criteria should consider whether:

* the approved delegation and its boundaries are recorded;
* relevant agent actions, tool interactions, material decisions, and outcomes
  can be reconstructed to the extent required by the context;
* records identify relevant human approvals, interventions, escalations, and
  changes to authority;
* records are protected against unauthorized alteration and are accessible
  to authorized reviewers;
* evidence is sufficiently complete, reliable, and timely for the intended
  assurance question; and
* evidence gaps and limitations are disclosed in the assessment record.

Potential evidence includes event logs, authorization records, agent and tool
interaction records, audit trails, incident records, integrity controls,
retention policies, and independent review reports.

A material gap exists where the organization cannot reconstruct a material
action or decision, cannot establish the authority under which it occurred, or
cannot provide reliable evidence for a critical control. Traceability alone
does not prove that a control was effective; assurance requires evaluation of
the evidence against the control or outcome being assessed.

4.15.8 Continuous Monitoring and Adaptation
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Assessment criteria should consider whether:

* monitoring objectives, indicators, thresholds, and owners are defined;
* material changes in performance, agent capability, tools, access, context,
  or organizational readiness can be detected;
* incidents, near misses, complaints, and control failures are captured and
  reviewed;
* reassessment triggers and review intervals are defined;
* there are operationally tested mechanisms to reduce, suspend, or terminate
  delegated authority; and
* lessons from monitoring and incidents lead to documented decisions and
  corrective action.

Potential evidence includes monitoring plans, dashboards, alert tests,
incident and near-miss records, review minutes, reassessment records, and
demonstrations of suspension or rollback procedures.

A material gap exists where material deterioration may remain undetected,
where alerts have no accountable recipient, or where the organization cannot
act on evidence that the delegation is no longer appropriate.

4.16 Cross-Cutting Decision Gates
---------------------------------

The assessment should not be reduced to an average across dimensions. A strong
result in one dimension does not offset a critical failure in another. The
following conditions should be treated as decision gates:

* **Authority gate:** the delegation objective, permitted actions, prohibited
  actions, limits, and approval authority are defined.
* **Capability gate:** relevant agent capability has been evaluated for the
  actual task and operating conditions, and material limitations are known.
* **Human control gate:** required human oversight, intervention, and
  escalation are practicable and have been tested.
* **Organizational control gate:** governance, operational support, incident
  response, and authority revocation are assigned and available.
* **Accountability gate:** critical responsibilities and decision rights have
  identifiable owners.
* **Evidence gate:** evidence needed to support the decision and reconstruct
  material activity is available at a level proportionate to the context.

A failed gate should result in deferral, reduction, or suspension of the
proposed delegation unless the issue is first remediated and reassessed. A
partially met or not-assessed critical criterion should be treated as an
unresolved gap, not silently converted into a pass.

The review authority should document the rationale for the overall decision,
including any accepted limitations, conditions, and residual uncertainties.
This gate approach is a proposed decision discipline and has not been
validated as a universal assessment method.

4.17 Evidence Quality and Assessment Confidence
-----------------------------------------------

Evidence should be evaluated for:

* **Relevance:** whether it addresses the specific criterion and delegation.
* **Currency:** whether it reflects the current agent, configuration,
  organization, and operating environment.
* **Completeness:** whether material periods, actions, roles, or conditions
  are missing.
* **Reliability:** whether the source and method provide a credible basis for
  the claim.
* **Independence:** whether evidence has been reviewed or corroborated by a
  party sufficiently independent of the activity, where appropriate.
* **Reproducibility:** whether the assessment or test can be repeated with
  sufficiently consistent results.
* **Limitations:** whether assumptions, exclusions, uncertainty, or known
  weaknesses are recorded.

Evidence should be linked to the criterion it supports. A policy document may
show that a process has been specified, but does not by itself demonstrate
that the process is implemented or effective. Operational records, tests,
interviews, and observed practice may be needed to corroborate documentary
evidence.

The assessor should distinguish evidence of design, implementation, and
operating effectiveness. These are different claims and should not be
collapsed into a single statement.

4.18 Accountability Role Allocation
-----------------------------------

The assessment should identify, at minimum, the following functions where
they are relevant to the delegation:

* **Delegation authority:** approves, limits, changes, or withdraws the
  delegated authority.
* **Delegating party:** defines the objective and grants authority within the
  approved governance process.
* **Operational owner:** is responsible for the operational service or
  process in which the agent acts.
* **Agent operator:** configures, operates, and maintains the agent and its
  integrations.
* **Supervising human or function:** monitors relevant activity and exercises
  oversight and intervention duties.
* **Incident owner:** coordinates response, investigation, and escalation
  for incidents involving the delegation.
* **Assurance or review function:** evaluates evidence and the continuing
  suitability of the delegation.

One person or function may hold more than one role where this is appropriate
and permitted by the organization's governance arrangements. The assessment
should nevertheless identify potential conflicts, capacity constraints, and
the need for independent review. The delegation authority should remain
identifiable; responsibility should not be assigned to the agent itself.

This role model is an organizational design aid. It does not prescribe a
universal organizational structure or determine legal accountability.
